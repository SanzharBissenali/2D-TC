# The fermionic toric code: dressing stars, breaking symmetry on purpose, and a frustration puzzle neither of us can explain

*2026-08-14, branch `fermionic-toric-code`*

This entry covers one session's worth of work on a new variant of the toric code, plus an unplanned detour into a genuinely open physics question that came out of comparing notes with a sibling project.

## The model

Take the ordinary 2D toric code: qubits on the links of a square lattice, vertex stabilizers `A_v = XXXX` and plaquette stabilizers `B_p = ZZZZ`. Now dress every vertex star with the plaquette diagonally to its upper-right:

```
A'_v = A_v · B_NE(v)
```

This is the Chen–Kapustin–Radičević exact-bosonization Gauss law (arXiv:1711.00515, Eq. 9) — we checked our construction against the actual paper text, not just the idea, including resolving an operator-ordering sign subtlety (they write the face operator before the star; we write it the other way; a vertex's star and its NE plaquette always share exactly two links, and swapping two anticommuting pairs past each other picks up `(-1)² = +1`, so both conventions land on the identical operator).

On the two links a star shares with its NE plaquette, `X` and `Z` now act on the *same* qubit. `X·Z = -iY` exactly, so the dressed operator is a real, six-body Pauli string — something like `-Y·Y·X·X·Z·Z` — with a genuine minus sign, not a modeling choice. Vertices with no NE plaquette (the top row and right column, under open boundary conditions) stay bare.

Two facts fall out immediately, and they matter for everything downstream:

- **Same stabilizer group.** Since `A'_v · B_NE(v) = A_v`, the group generated by `{A'_v, B_p}` is identical to the group generated by `{A_v, B_p}`. At zero field, the ground state of the fermionic toric code *is* the ground state of the ordinary toric code — positive, unique, `E₀ = -(#stabilizers)`. At L=4 that's `E₀ = -25` exactly, and it's not even a new calculation: it's already sitting in `results/ed/ed_L4_hx0.00_hz0.00.json` from a previous experiment on the plain model.
- **Different excitations.** The open-boundary relation `∏_v A'_v = ∏_p B_p` ties magnetic and electric parity together, so a single flux excitation is forbidden — excitations pair up. The gap is 4, not 2. That's real new physics even though the ground state is unchanged.

## Building it, and then trying to break it

The geometry and Hamiltonian code (`model/geometry.py::_generate_dressed_stars`, `model/hamiltonian.py`'s `ftc` branch) turned out to be almost embarrassingly simple to write correctly: build the dressed operator as a plain product of X-factors then Z-factors, and the `-iY` and the overall `-1` fall out of ordinary Pauli algebra with zero manual sign bookkeeping. The hard part was making sure "simple to write" didn't mean "simple to get subtly wrong."

So before trusting any of it, six independent agents went after the implementation adversarially — each given only primary sources (the raw diagrams, the actual CKR paper, the code) and explicitly told not to assume any answer was correct going in. Between them they:

- Re-derived the dressed-star construction from scratch in fresh numpy (not reusing our own validator) and checked it against the original hand-drawn diagram and the actual arXiv paper text.
- Independently rebuilt the Hamiltonian and diagonalized it at small L, confirming `E₀ = -(#stabilizers)`, gap 4 vs. 2, and the group-identity `A'_v · B_NE(v) = A_v` as an *exact matrix equation* — zero commutator, not an inference.
- Traced the entire `--ftc` flag end-to-end through the codebase looking for wiring gaps.
- Re-derived the parameter-count formulas for every architecture from the raw layer code, catching and correcting a stale number in our own documentation along the way (a param count we'd been calling "1233" turned out to be one block's count, not the network total — the real total was 1681).
- Ran the numpy validator and the analysis tooling against synthetic and real data to make sure they weren't circular.

They found exactly two real bugs, both fixed before anything ran on real hardware: the `--ftc` exclusivity check didn't block `hy`, even though `hy` breaks the dressed-star symmetry exactly the way `hx` does (a second, unvalidated fermionic cut that would otherwise train silently); and a boundary-sentinel filter that was dormant today but structurally inconsistent with the rest of the codebase. Everything else passed, including an uncomfortable number of places where it would have been easy to get a sign or an index wrong.

## The actual experiment: does exact symmetry matter even with no sign problem?

The interesting question wasn't "does the network find the ground state at h=0" — an identity-initialized Combo network is *constructed* to start exactly there, before a single training step. That's a wiring check, not a test of anything.

The real test: build a second, genuinely unconstrained architecture — a plain stack of the same local convolutional layers, but with no Wilson nonlinearity and no invariant block, no architectural symmetry of any kind — give it comparable capacity (1800 parameters vs. the symmetric network's 1681), the same optimizer, same sampler, same step budget, and see whether it can find the *same* ground state on its own. At the trivial h=0 point. Where there's no sign problem, no frustration, nothing exotic — just a highly entangled topologically-ordered state that happens to be exactly representable by a positive amplitude.

It couldn't.

| | symmetric (`cnn`) | unconstrained (`plaincnn`) |
|---|---|---|
| final energy | −25.000000 | −23.45 |
| relative error vs. exact | **1.47 × 10⁻⁸** | **6.22 × 10⁻²** |
| V-score | ~10⁻¹¹ | ~1.4 |

Four million times worse, and not because anything broke. Training was stable throughout — no NaN, no divergence, small per-step energy fluctuations. The unconstrained network just drifted toward a different, partially-polarized state instead: its per-site magnetization along X sat around 0.1–0.16 (should be ≈0 in the true ground state), and its stabilizer diagnostics were informatively uneven — it had mostly recovered the plain vertex-star structure (`⟨A_v⟩ ≈ 0.9996`) but not the plaquette structure (`⟨B_p⟩ ≈ 0.913`) or the full dressed-star structure (`⟨A'_v⟩ ≈ 0.951`). It found *part* of the answer and stalled.

The takeaway: exact symmetry embedding isn't only a tool for taming sign problems. Here, with no sign problem anywhere in sight, it's still doing real work — it's what lets local optimization actually reach a topologically non-trivial state instead of getting stuck near it.

## A detour: comparing notes with the 3D version of this problem, and finding something neither of us expected

There's a sibling project working the analogous 3D fermionic toric code, which had fought a genuinely hard sign problem at its own zero-field point — hard enough that they'd built a whole analytic "sign head" to handle it, frozen outside the optimizer, because stochastic reconfiguration has no mechanism to discover a discrete sign structure on its own; it can only reweight amplitudes smoothly.

When they heard our `cnn` arm landed on the exact ground energy "for free," the first hypothesis was dimensional: maybe our sign structure sits in a combinatorially tractable regime (something Kasteleyn-adjacent) that theirs doesn't. That hypothesis didn't survive five minutes of scrutiny — our h=0 point isn't sign-problem-full at all, it's *provably* positive, and the network starts there by construction. No optimization fought anything. Once we cleared that up, the conversation moved to the actually-interesting question sitting one field-strength away: does the perturbed, genuinely non-stoquastic Hamiltonian (`hx`-field on) also stay sign-free?

Small-L exact diagonalization said yes — suspiciously cleanly. Every ground-state amplitude stayed positive across the whole scanned window at both L=2 and L=3. That's when it got strange. Building the L=2 Hamiltonian by hand and checking it directly, we found:

- The Hamiltonian is **genuinely, provably frustrated** — not just "looks non-stoquastic." A three-cycle among basis configurations has an odd number of positive edges, a textbook unfixable obstruction in the signed-graph sense (Harary balance). We confirmed this exhaustively: brute-forcing all 65,536 possible diagonal sign gauges at L=2, none of them makes the Hamiltonian entrywise non-positive off-diagonal. This is not gauge-equivalent to a stoquastic Hamiltonian, full stop — not a heuristic, a computed fact.
- The frustrated configurations carry real weight in the ground state (comparable to the largest amplitudes present), not some corner the state quietly avoids.
- And yet the ground state stays exactly positive anyway. Confirmed at both L=2 and L=3.

We chased down *why* the frustration exists, at least: every off-diagonal matrix element comes from an X-flip (Z is diagonal, so it never contributes one). Bare stars and the field term are pure X-products, and a product of Paulis-X has matrix element exactly +1 between a configuration and its flip — always, unconditionally, no matter the configuration. So their contribution to that Hamiltonian entry is always negative. Dressed stars are different: because they're built X-content-then-Z-content, the Z part evaluates first, on the *pre-flip* configuration, contributing that generator's own plaquette flux eigenvalue — a genuinely configuration-dependent ±1. So the sign structure lives entirely in the dressed stars: bare stars and fields literally cannot ever produce a positive matrix element, and every positive entry anywhere in the Hamiltonian traces back to a dressed star's own flux. The sibling project verified this exhaustively too — zero exceptions, at both system sizes.

What we don't have is an explanation for the actual puzzle: a Hamiltonian that's provably, substantially, unfixably frustrated, whose ground state is nonetheless exactly positive. That's not the standard Marshall-sign-rule story (there's no gauge doing the work), and it's not a small-L accident either, since it survives the jump from L=2 to L=3 intact. It might be some other symmetry the ground state respects; it might be specific to this bosonization construction and already known in a corner of the literature neither of us has found yet. Genuinely open.

Practically, this pushes toward not needing a complex-valued network or a sign head for the perturbed cut — but we're holding that call for an actual L=4 confirmation rather than extrapolating from L=2/L=3, since L=4 is a different symmetry-sector-counting regime, not just a bigger copy of the same thing. That's a cheap next experiment (one exact-diagonalization point, real dtype, a few minutes) and it's the natural next step before running the field sweep for real.

## Where this leaves things

- The fermionic toric code implementation is done, adversarially reviewed, and empirically validated end to end — the h=0 wiring check and the real ED companion both landed exactly where the math said they should.
- The architecture comparison delivered a clean, unambiguous result: exact symmetry embedding matters even when there's no sign problem to hide behind.
- The perturbed (`hx`) cut opened a real physics question that neither of two independent research efforts can currently answer, and that's worth sitting with rather than rushing past.
- Next: one small ED point at L=4 to decide whether the field sweep needs a complex ansatz at all — and then the sweep itself.
